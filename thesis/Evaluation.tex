\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

As part of this research, an experiment was conducted on human participants,
in which they interacted with the college course RS.
The results of this experiment are evaluated in this chapter.
The full methodology of the experiment will be described,
as well as an analysis of the experiment results.

\section{Experiment Methodology}

This section will describe the complete methodology behind the experiment conducted in order to 
evaluate this dissertation's RS.
We will describe the ethics behind the research, the sample of the experiment,
and the experiment itself.

\subsection{Research Ethics}

The proposed experiment was approved by the Trinity College Dublin research ethics committee.
Prior to beginning the experiment, participants read a participant information leaflet,
which provided information about the experiment and what it would entail,
and they read an informed consent form.
If participants were willing to take part in the experiment, they signed the informed consent form.
All data collected during this experiment was anonymised with no traceable link back to the participant.

\subsection{Experiment Sample}

Seeing as our RS is focused on college course recommendations in the Irish context,
we sought participants that were over the ages of 18, and are currently enrolled in a college course in Ireland.
According to \cite{hea2024key}, the experiment population size is about 250,000.
No direct benefits or payments were provided to participants.
As part of the experiment, participants provided their original CAO college course list
that they applied to prior to their first year of study.
An example of this CAO list is given in figure \ref{fig:cao-course-list-methodology} below:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering

    \caption{An example of an NFQ Level 8 CAO college course list.}
    \label{fig:cao-course-list-methodology}
\end{figure}

Participants that fit the above requirements were sought opportunistically,
and a sample size of $n=31$ was obtained for this experiment.

\subsection{The Experiment}

After the participant voluntarily accepted to participate in the study, 
experiments were either conducted in person. or over online video conferencing software.
The PI was present constantly, and the participant was made aware that they can ask questions at any time.
From start to finish, the experiments took no longer than 60 minutes.

The first part of the experiment was for the user to interact with the college course RS's user interface,
as given in \href{https://docs.google.com/forms/d/e/1FAIpQLSdTPTCry6UxXJyUvBKZ13pgW1ETBkdk_l7sm0K3k2sVo8TAXg/viewform?usp=dialog}{\underline{this}} Google Form.
This involved the user filling out their expected LC points, NFQ level and college location preferences,
as shown in subsection \ref{sec:kbf}.
As well as this, the user answered the questions regarding their academic interests and RIASEC traits,
as shown in subsection \ref{sec:examples-quiz}.

Upon completion of the quiz, the PI ran the recommendation
algorithm on the user's quiz results.
This produced a .pdf with the user's 20 college course recommendations, which was then shared with the user.
As will be described in section \ref{sec:baseline-rs}, a baseline college course recommendation algorithm was designed as part of this experiment.
As a result, the .pdf file contains 20 college course recommendations generated from this dissertation's RS algorithm,
and 20 college course recommendations generated by the baseline algorithm.
The participants were told that one set of recommendations was generated by this dissertation's RS,
and the other set was generated by an anonymous algorithm.
The participant was unaware which recommendations were the baseline or the actual algorithm.
The recommendation sets are simply denoted as `set 1' and `set 2', as show in figure \ref{fig:rec-sets} below:

\section{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}

Recall that this dissertation's research question is: \emph{``Can a Recommender System assist an Irish student in exploring college courses they would consider studying?"},
and we hypothesised that an RS that provides relevant, diverse and serendipitous recommendations would answer this research question.
In order to measure the effectiveness of our answer to this research question,
we need measurable evaluation metrics, and this section will describe the evaluation metrics for this dissertation.
It is worth mentioning that evaluation metrics are gathered for both the baseline and actual RS.

\emph{Note:} in this chapter, when we say that a user `likes' a college course recommendation, 
this is equivalent to saying that a user `would realistically consider studying' the college course recommendation - 
this is done for brevity.

\subsection{User-perceived Diversity}

Diversity is one of the qualities of an effective RS.
Objectively measuring diversity in the context of college course recommendations is challenging 
and may differ according to the end user's interpretation.
As a result, we decided to opt for user-perceived, or subjective, diversity.
This was evaluated by asking the user the question shown in figure \ref{fig:diversity-question} below:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\fbox{\includegraphics[scale=0.45]{images/diversity-question.png}}
	\caption{Asking the user for their user-perceived diversity of recommendation as part of the experiment.}
	\label{fig:diversity-question}
\end{figure}

As a result, the user-perceived diversity metric is an integer between 1 and 5, where 1 implies low diversity and 5 implies high diversity.

\subsection{User-perceived Trust}

In \cite{aggarwal2016recommender}, an additional quality of an effective RS is a user's trust in the recommendations
and the system behind it.
Naturally, measuring trust objectively is not possible as it is user-dependant.
As a result, we will measure the user's trust subjectively in a similar manner to user-perceived diversity.
We evaluated the user's perceived trust in the RS by asking the user the question shown in figure \ref{fig:trust-question} below:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\fbox{\includegraphics[scale=0.45]{images/trust-question.png}}
	\caption{Asking the user for their user-perceived trust of recommendation as part of the experiment.}
	\label{fig:trust-question}
\end{figure}

As a result, the user-perceived trust metric is a integer between 1 and 5, where 1 implies low trust and 5 implies high trust.

\subsection{Precision}

Precision is a common metric in RS problems. 
Recall that users evaluated whether or not they would realistically consider studying each recommended college 
course in figure \ref{fig:eval-quiz}.
In the context of college course recommendations, precision can be verbally defined as: \emph{``What proportion of the 20 college 
course recommendations did the user like?".}
In the context of our problem, precision is expressed mathematically as:

\[ 
\text{Precision}\;=\;\frac{\#\;\text{Liked college course recommendations}}{20}
\]

\subsection{Recall}

Recall is also a common metric in RS problems.
In the context of our problem, recall can be defined as: \emph{``Of all the college courses that the user likes, what proportion was recommended by the RS?''}.
Naturally, obtaining a truly representative set of `all the college courses that the user likes'
would require the user to go through all $\sim$1740 college courses in the dataset, 
and asking the user if they like the college course or not.
However, this would be a very time-consuming and mentally draining task.
As a result, we would not consider this approach feasible in the context of an experiment.

The original CAO list the user provides would not be considered a set of `all the college courses that the user likes',
because this constrains the list to 10 college courses, when a user could theoretically like more than 10 college courses.
Due to these limitations, we have decided not to include recall as a metric in the context of college course recommendations.

\subsection{Serendipity}

Serendipity is a recommendation that is both useful and unexpected to the user.
In the Irish context, a user's CAO list is a representation of the user's known, relevant college courses.
Following this reasoning, a college course that is recommended to the user that is not present in their CAO list 
can be seen as serendipitous.
To be more specific to the context of this problem, serendipity can be defined as: \emph{``What proportion of the 20 college course recommendations did the user like AND was not in their CAO list?''}.
Serendipity can also be mathematically expressed as:

\[ 
\text{Serendipity}\;=\;\frac{\text{\# (Liked college course recommendations NOT in user CAO List)}}{20}
\]

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics Summary}

From the above investigations, we have now gathered and defined four measurable evaluation metrics.
As described by \cite{aggarwal2016recommender}, the three qualities to an effective RS are: relevance, diversity and
serendipity.
Aggarwal et al. also described trust as an additional quality of an effective RS,
meaning we have four qualities of consideration when it comes to an effective RS.

We can measure each of these four qualities with our established four metrics, 
as is more clearly defined in table \ref{tab:rs-quality-metrics} below:

\section{Baseline RS}
\label{sec:baseline-rs}

In isolation, providing this dissertation's RS performance on the established evaluation metrics
does not provide significant information.
Generally in RS problems, the performance is compared to a baseline RS.
This section will describe the implementation of an objective baseline RS in the context 
of Irish college course recommendations.
The implementation is intentionally straightforward and reproducible,
so others may easily implement the baseline algorithm should they approach a similar problem to this dissertation.

As mentioned in section \ref{sec:lc-points}, the LC points associated with a course are a reflection of the 
demand of the course. As a result,
an appropriate baseline in the context of Irish college course recommendations would 
simply be an RS that provides a list of college course sorted descending by LC points.
So, the baseline RS would return college courses from 625 LC points and below.

However, this baseline RS may be incredibly irrelevant to some users.
For example, it would be unfair for the baseline to recommend 625-point courses 
to users that expect to receive 300 LC points.
As well as this, it would be unfair for the baseline to recommend 
college courses in Trinity College Dublin to a user that is only interested 
in studying in University of Limerick,
or NFQ level 8 college courses to a user that is only interested in studying NFQ level 6 courses.

As a result, in the interest of a fairness, the baseline RS will filter college courses by the user's specified 
college preferences, expected LC points and NFQ levels.
After this filtering, the baseline RS will return the top 20 college courses sorted descending by LC 
points.
If there is a tie in LC points, the tied courses should be sorted alphabetically.

With the baseline RS established, we can observe the results of the experiment.

\subsection{Statistical Significance of Results}
\label{sec:statistical-sig}

Recall research objective \ref{res-obj-statistically}: ``Create a college course RS with statistically significant improvements compared to a baseline college course RS''.
In the previous subsection, we observed the actual RS improving upon the baseline's performance
across all four evaluation metrics.
However, we have yet to analyse whether or not these results are statistically significant,
which is the objective of this subsection.
\emph{Note:} we will work with a standard 95\% level of statistical confidence in this analysis.

\subsubsection{Measuring the Normality of Evaluation Metrics}

First, we must determine if our four gathered evaluation metrics come from a normal distribution.
We can use the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality, with a null hypothesis that the data follow a normal distribution.
If the $p$ value is $\le 0.05$, we reject the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative 
hypothesis, which claims the data no not follow a normal distribution.
Seeing as our experiment involves the same user being evaluated by the actual and baseline RS,
for each metric we will run the Shapiro-Wilk test on the differences between the actual and baseline,
with the results of our test shown in table \ref{tab:shapiro-wilk} below:

\begin{table}[H]
\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
	\hline
 	\textbf{Evaluation Metric} & \textbf{Shapiro-Wilk p-value} & \textbf{Is Normally Distributed?}\\
  	\hline
	Precision & 0.60 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	Diversity & 0.23 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	Serendipity & 0.93 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	Trust & 0.023 & \ding{55}\\
	\hline
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\caption{Results of the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality on our four evaluation metrics.}
\label{tab:shapiro-wilk}
\end{table}

From observing the results of table \ref{tab:shapiro-wilk}, all of our metrics follow a normal distribution
except for the trust metric.

\subsubsection{Investigating Significant Statistical Differences between Samples}

If the examined metric follows a normal distribution, we can use a paired t-test (PTT) to 
determine a statistically significant difference between the sample means.
Otherwise, we can use a Wilcoxon signed-rank test (WSRT), which evaluates the median differences between the samples.
With a $p$ value of $\le 0.05$, we have sufficient evidence to reject the null hypothesis, that means/medians of both 
samples are equal, in favour of the alternative hypothesis, that the means/medians of two samples differ significantly.
The results of this test is shown in table \ref{tab:two-sample-test} below:

\begin{table}[H]
\begin{center}
	\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|p{7cm}|}
	\hline
 	\textbf{Evaluation Metric} & \textbf{Test type} & \textbf{p-value} & \textbf{Significant Difference Between Means/Medians?}\\
  	\hline
	Precision & PTT & $<$0.001 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	Diversity & PTT & 0.58 & \ding{55}\\
	\hline
	Serendipity & PTT & $<$0.001 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	Trust & WSRT & $<$0.001 & \ding{51}\\
	\hline
	\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\caption{Results of the PTT or WSRT on our four evaluation metrics.}
\label{tab:two-sample-test}
\end{table}

From observing the results of table \ref{tab:two-sample-test} above,
we have sufficient evidence that the means of precision, serendipity and trust 
differ significantly from the actual RS compared to the baseline.
On the other hand, we do not have sufficient evidence to reject the hypothesis that the means 
of diversity differ significantly from the actual compared to the baseline.

\subsubsection{Measuring the Magnitude of Difference between Sample Means}

Seeing we have evidence that three out of four of our evaluation metrics differ significantly from the actual 
compared to the baseline, it is also useful to see how much the means of the samples differ.
For normally distributed samples, we can use the Cohen effect size, $d$,
a measure of difference between two sample means.
According to \cite{cohen2013statistical}, $d\ge0.8$ is considered a `large' difference between sample means,
$d\ge0.5$ is a `medium' difference,
and $d\ge0.2$ is a `small' difference.
We can visually display our Cohen effect size, $d$, for our three normally-distributed evaluation metrics in figure \ref{fig:cohen-effect-size} below:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.6]{images/cohen-effect-size.png}
    \caption{The Cohen effect size, $d$, of our three normally-distributed evaluation metrics.}
    \label{fig:cohen-effect-size}
\end{figure}

From observing figure \ref{fig:cohen-effect-size} above, 
the Cohen effect size for precision and serendipity all lie 
well above the `large' threshold of 0.8, at 1.34 and 1.11 respectively,
indicating a large magnitude of difference between the sample means of the actual and the baseline.
On the other hand when considering diversity, a value of $d=0.1$ indicates a negligible increase from the actual compared 
to the baseline.

Seeing as the trust metric is not normally distributed and we used the WSRT, 
we can use the Matched-pairs rank biserial correlation (MPRSC) to 
to calculate the effect size $r$.
According to \cite{cohen2013statistical}, $r\ge0.5$ is considered a `large' difference,
$r\ge0.3$ is a `medium' difference, and $r\ge0.1$ is a `small' difference.
Using the MPRSC, the effect size for the trust metric is calculated as $r=0.96$.
This value of $r$ lies well above the 0.5 threshold, indicating a large magnitude of difference 
between the actual and the baseline ranks for the trust metric.

\subsection{Summary}

Recall research objective \ref{res-obj-statistically}: ``Create a college course RS with statistically significant improvements compared to a baseline college course RS'',
and our four evaluation metrics: diversity, trust, precision and serendipity.
This section demonstrated large, statistically significant improvements 
for the actual RS compared to the baseline for the three metrics:
trust, precision and serendipity,
which is a satisfactory result.
That being said, no statistically significant improvement was found for the diversity metric.
As a result, we do not technically consider this research objective fully achieved,
as the actual RS was unable to produce statistically significant improvements 
for the diversity metric.

However as mentioned earlier, it is a very difficult task for an actual RS to outperform the baseline in terms of diversity,
because the baseline RS only recommends courses 
sorted by LC points, and thus the recommendations will be very diverse by nature.
Seeing that the actual RS has similar performance to the baseline in terms of diversity,
we would arguably see this as a success.
While the original research objective was not technically, fully achieved,
three metrics had significant improvements and our diversity metric was competitive 
with a baseline that is very diverse by nature .
Thus, we can consider the actual RS to demonstrate satisfactory performance in the context of research objective \ref{res-obj-statistically}.

\subsection{Limitations of Academic Interests Labels}

As seen in table \ref{tab:academic-interest-categories}, `languages' is one of the academic interests.
Some users in our experiments demonstrated interests in languages,
and in one case the user was recommended Law and French from Trinity College Dublin.
This user did not know any French, and instead was more interested in a language like Irish or German.

interesting edge case, and in the context of college course recommendations,

\subsection{The Role of Subjectivity in Answering the Quiz}

Despite the question being phrased as: \emph{``I would like to do this activity for a living''},
one participant approached the quiz differently.
They were well-aware that the intention of the quiz was to provide college course recommendations.
This participant also highly valued salary, so they only highly rated questions 
when the job behind the activity was high-paying.
After answering the quiz in this way, 
the participant received recommendations in high-paying fields which aligned with their original desires,
resulting in satisfactory performance.

Even without knowing the algorithm behind the RS, most RS's work on the same high-level idea:
``recommend items according to the user's highly-ranked responses".
In the case of a film RS like Netflix, if a user positively rates comedy films, 
they can expect to receive comedy recommendations.
Arguably, a user without a technical background would understand this simple, high-level idea.

In section \ref{sec:college-course-justifications},
we discovered that users have many different values and justifications for choosing their college course.
In a way,
the user can construct their own way of answering the quiz
and tune the recommendations to align with their values.
For example, a user that values passion in their college course can only highly rate questions 
when the activity involves something they love, like art or music.
Or a user that values social impact can only highly rate questions when the activity involves something 
meaningful, like social policy or the environment.
In this manner, regardless of how the question is phrased (\emph{``I would like to do this activity for a living''}),
users can still answer the quiz in a way that aligns with their values,
which is different to every individual.
