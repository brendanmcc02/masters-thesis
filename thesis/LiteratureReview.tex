\section{Fundamentals of college course choice}
\label{section:fundamentals}

% TODO - probably need to re-write this after changing chapter content!

This section provides a broad overview into the fundamentals of college course choice.
Ultimately, this discussions seeks to provide insights to the question: \emph{"How do people pick college courses?"}.
As we will discover in the coming sections, there are no straightforward answers to this question, 
and relevant nuances will be discussed.
% TODO - not so happy with the phrasing of this, and ultimately the significance the foundations that are layed in this chapter
Understanding the fundamentals of college course choice is important to this dissertation for two main reasons:
firstly, to help us answer the question: \emph{"What relevant information should the user be questioned in order to make suitable college course recommendations?"},
which will be explored in Section \ref{section:relevant}.
Secondly, to ensure that design decisions are well-informed by the literature surrounding college course choice, which will be explored in Chapter \ref{chapter:design}.\\

As established in Chapter \ref{chapter:introduction}, choosing a college course is a major life decision.
As a result, we can assume that students choose a particular course at a particular college for a set of reasons.
Some of these reasons are intrinsic preferences to the individual, and others are external, societal influences.
We will first examine a student's intrinsic preferences behind their college course choice.\\

\subsection{Individual preferences and motivations}
\label{section:individual}

% TODO - probably need a mini-intro here! tell the reader what they are going to read, but first look at the intro of this chapter first and refine that!

According to \cite{matthews2024students, gillis2021choosing, mullen2011degrees}, the most common and prominent reasons students cited when
choosing their college course can be broadly categorised as: \emph{interest} or \emph{passion} in the subject area,
\emph{helping people} or \emph{making a difference},
\emph{satisfying parent's desires},
\emph{work/life balance},
and \emph{salary expectations} and \emph{career prospects} after graduation.
This is by no means an exhaustive list, however, it does cover the most prominent reasons provided by students 
when justifying their college course choice.\\

These reasons were all commonly cited across genders and race/ethnicity, 
however, there were different priorities across gender and race/ethnicity.
For instance, \cite{gillis2021choosing} found that on average, men are more likely to be career-oriented 
when choosing their college course compared to women, whereas in \cite{matthews2024students}, men were found to be more likely to cite \emph{salary expectations} and \emph{career prospects}
as their reasons for choosing their college course.
Moreover, \cite{goyette2006studies} found that Asians were more likely to 
prioritise \emph{salary expectations} in their college course choice, compared to other races/ethnicities.
The above examples showcase the different priorities of reasons for college course choice across race/ethnicity and gender.
Despite this difference,
the papers still conclude that all the above reasons are relevant across the differing genders and races/ethnicities.
% probably could phrase this better, quite confusingly-worded
In other words, despite the findings that, for example, Asians were more likely to prioritise \emph{salary expectations} after graduation, 
does not imply Asians do not also value other motivations in choosing their college course, such as \emph{interest or passion} in the subject.\\

\subsection{College preferences}
\label{section:college-influences}

Choosing a college course is one decision, but a separate and closely related decision is choosing the college itself.
In this subsection, there will be an examination into a student's preferences for college choice.\\

For example, a student may settle on studying Computer Science, but the next question to ask is: \emph{Where do I study Computer Science?}
\cite{oya2015university, cmp2018students, baharun2010institutional} have found that students provide a variety of reasons for their college choice, the most prominent reasons include:
\emph{prestige} or \emph{reputation} of the college, 
\emph{location} or \emph{practicality}, 
\emph{friends} attending the college,
\emph{departments} or \emph{academic staff},
\emph{family members} being college alumni,
and particular \emph{sports clubs} or \emph{societies}.
Once again, this is by no means an exhaustive list, however, it does cover the most prominent reasons provided by students
when justifying their college choice.\\

That being said, there seem to be cultural differences 
in priorities towards college choice.
For instance, in \cite{baharun2010institutional}, a study of Malaysian college students, the college's \emph{reputation} was the most important criteria
in their college choice justification,
whereas in \cite{telli2006university}, a study of Turkish college students, \emph{departments} and \emph{academic staff} were the most important criteria
in their college course justification.
While we can clearly observe cultural differences in the most important criteria for college choice,
a similar point stands:
just because Malaysian students, for example, value college \emph{reputation} as the most important criteria in college choice, 
does not imply Malaysian students do not value other aspects to the college when reasoning about their college decision, 
as can be found in \cite{baharun2010institutional}.\\

\subsubsection{Parent's educational and financial background}
% TODO - might need a mini-intro here
In \cite{leighton2023rich}, there is a strong correlation between a student's college course choice and their parent's educational background.
According to their findings, students with more educated parents will lean towards college courses with lower early-career earnings,
but with faster growth towards higher earnings later in their career.
This is likely due to greater \emph{financial risk tolerance} - students with more affluent backgrounds can afford to pursue college courses with lower early-career earnings,
and defer gratification for higher earnings later on in their career.
On the other hand, according to \cite{leighton2023rich}, students with less educated parents will lean towards college courses with higher early-career earnings
and a more defined career paths.
This is likely due to lower \emph{financial risk tolerance}; students with less affluent backgrounds have less opportunity to afford deferred gratification,
and as a result, they prioritise "safer" college courses with more defined career paths that yield higher early-career earnings.
There are analogous findings in \cite{mullen2011degrees}: working class parents generally encourage their children to attend college courses 
that directly prepare them for careers, whereas middle class parents generally encourage college courses where their children can explore their interests.
Moreover, \cite{mullen2003goes} has shown that the parental educational background of an individual has a strong correlation with 
the individual's attendance in a postgraduate degree program.
The findings above all give credence to the idea that an individual's college course choice is strongly influenced by both their parent's educational and financial backgrounds.\\

Interestingly, according to \cite{leighton2023rich}, they failed to find a strong correlation between the parental income of a student 
and their college course choice.
That being said, they did find a strong correlation between the parental income of a student and the college they study at.
It is worth mentioning this study was conducted on data obtained from the United States Census Bureau 2009-2018 survey \footnote{\url{https://www.census.gov/programs-surveys/acs}}.
According to \cite{college2025pricing}, college undergraduate fees in the USA can range from \$4,150 - \$45,000 annually,
resulting in a much larger barrier to entry for American students from less affluent backgrounds.
As discussed in Section \ref{section:college-course-fees}, the majority of Irish undergraduate college courses
are priced at \euro 2,500 annually, a more affordable price for undergraduate education compared to the USA.
This lower price not only lowers the barrier of entry for Irish students from less affluent backgrounds,
there are also higher education grants \footnote{\url{https://www.susi.ie/}} for Irish students with income underneath a certain threshold.\\

That being said, it would be ignorant to assume that parental income is not an influence on college choice for Irish students.
For example, according to the QS World University Rankings 2026 \footnote{\url{https://www.topuniversities.com/world-university-rankings?countries=ie}},
the two top-ranked universities in Ireland are in Dublin.
According to \cite{tudublin2025cost}, it is estimated to cost a student an additional \euro4,000 - \euro11,000 every year to live away from home in 
Dublin, compared to someone who lives in Dublin at a family home, clearly demonstrating a limitation imposed 
on Irish students who cannot afford that kind of money.
As a result, we can clearly demonstrate that financial background has an influence on the college choice of Irish students.

\subsubsection{Gender}

In this discussion we will explore the influence of gender on college course choice.
Figure \ref{fig:gender-diff} visualises the proportional difference of gender across different fields of study in Irish colleges.
As is clearly observable, many fields of study are not equal in gender.
To give some extreme examples of college course participation in Ireland, according to \cite{hea2024key}, Fisheries has 100\% male participation, and Pre-school education has 97\% female participation,
demonstrating the large gaps in gender proportions across different fields of study in Ireland.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.42]{images/gender-differences.png}
    \caption{Mean gender differences across different fields of study in Irish colleges. Red=Male, Blue=Female, Green=Non-binary or Prefer not to say. Source: \cite{hea2024key}.}
    \label{fig:gender-diff}
\end{figure}

The above Figure \ref{fig:gender-diff} and aforementioned statistics clearly demonstrate gender differences across different fields of study,
and the next question to ask is: "what are the negative impacts, if any, of studying a college course that 
features a strong proportion of one gender?" \cite{slattery2023navigating} conducted a qualitative study on 
21 female students studying either Mathematics (66\% male), Physics (68\% male), Computer Science (74\% male) or Engineering (71\% male)
in Ireland - parenthesised data is from \cite{hea2024key}.
\cite{slattery2023navigating} found that during their studies, females felt under-valued by their male peers,
which negatively impacted their self-beliefs and interest in the course subject matter.
In addition to this, they found that females suffered more pressure to perform, 
and were less likely to engage in the learning environment,
showcasing an overall negative experience for females in male-dominated fields of study.
\\

Furthermore, in \cite{iwish2025survey}, a survey of Irish secondary school girl's attitudes towards Science, Technology, Engineering and Mathematics (STEM),
65\% of girls reported that "Poor gender equality in STEM careers" is a barrier to continuing their studies in STEM,
demonstrating the strong influence of gender in college course choice in Ireland.

\subsubsection{Race and Ethnicity}

In this discussion, we will explore the influence of Race and Ethnicity on college course choice.
The author was unable to find statistics for racial or ethnic differences by college course study in Ireland,
so statistics from \cite{racial2015peter} are used, which was collected from the National Center for Education Statistics in the US Department of Education.\\

According to analysis from Hinrichs, there are a handful of striking racial or ethnic differences by field of study in college.
For example, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:stem-diff}, 31\% of Asian students study a STEM-related college course,
a much higher proportion compared to 11\% black, 13\% hispanic and 15\% white students.
An additional example of racial or ethnic differences by field of study can be found in Figure \ref{fig:public-admin-diff},
which examines the proportion of race and ethnicity in Public Administration and Social Service.
Nearly 4\% of black students study in this field, a relatively large difference compared to under 1\% for Asian,
2.5\% hispanic and 1.5\% white students studying in this field.

Moreover, we can examine the analysis conducted by \cite{dickson2010race}, a study performed on administrative data from three Texas Universities (n=127,330).
Similar proportions of findings were reported, for example it was also found that 
37\% of STEM students were Asian, with 26\% white, 24\% black and 23\% hispanic students being the other prominent racial/ethnic groups.
As to why this may be the case, \cite{xie2003social} argues that Asian-Americans prioritise "objective" credentials and "hard" skills, 
which are more difficult to discriminate against in job interviews that are more focused on technical ability, as opposed to job interviews focusing more on "soft" skills.
This poses a potential explanation for the relatively hgh proportion of Asians in STEM compared to other racial/ethnic groups.\\

race or ethnicity across college course choices in the USA.
There was particular focus on the high proportion of Asians in STEM,
for which \cite{xie2003social} provided a potential explanation,
ultimately demonstrating the influence of race/ethnicity on an individual's college course choice.
