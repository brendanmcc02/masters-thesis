\section{Fundamentals of college course choice}
\label{section:fundamentals}

This section provides a broad overview into the fundamentals of college course choice.
Ultimately, this discussions seeks to provide insights to the question: \emph{"How do people pick college courses?"}.
As we will discover in the coming sections, there are no straightforward answers to this question, 
and there are many nuances which will be discussed.
% TODO - not so happy with the phrasing of this, and ultimately the significance the foundations that are layed in this chapter
Understanding the fundamentals of college course choice is important to this dissertation for two main reasons:
firstly, to help us answer the question: \emph{"What relevant information should the user be questioned in order to make suitable college course recommendations?"},
which will be explored in Section \ref{section:relevant}.
Secondly, to ensure that design decisions are well-informed by the literature surrounding college course choice, which will be explored in Chapter \ref{chapter:design}.\\

As established in Chapter \ref{chapter:introduction}, choosing a college course is a major life decision.
As a result, we can assume that students choose a particular course at a particular college for a set of reasons.
Some of these reasons are intrinsic preferences to the individual, and others are external, societal influences.
We will first examine a student's intrinsic preferences behind their college course choice.\\

\subsection{Individual preferences and motivations}
\label{section:individual}



In \cite{matthews2024students, gillis2021choosing, mullen2011degrees}, the most common and prominent reasons students cited when
choosing their college course can be broadly categorised as: \emph{interest} or \emph{passion} in the subject area,
\emph{helping people} or \emph{making a difference},
\emph{satisfying parent's desires},
\emph{work/life balance},
and \emph{salary expectations} and \emph{career prospects} after graduation.
These reasons were all commonly cited across genders and race, 
however, there were different priorities across gender and race.
For instance, \cite{gillis2021choosing} found that on average, men are more likely to be career-oriented 
when choosing their college course compared to women, whereas in \cite{matthews2024students}, men were found to be more likely to cite \emph{salary expectations} and \emph{career prospects}
as their reasons for choosing their college course.
Moreover, \cite{goyette2006studies} found that those of Asian descent were more likely to 
prioritise \emph{salary expectations} in their college course choice,
showcasing the different priorities of reasons for college course choice across race and gender.
Despite a difference in priorities across gender and race when considering college course choice,
the papers still conclude all the above reasons are relevant across the differing genders and races.
In other words, despite the findings that those of Asian descent were more likely to prioritise salary expectations after graduation, 
does not imply they do not also value other motivations in choosing their college course, such as \emph{interest or passion} in the subject, or \emph{helping people}.\\

\subsection{College influences}
\label{section:college-influences}

Choosing a college course is one decision, but a separate and closely related decision is choosing the college itself.
For instance, a student may settle on studying Computer Science, but the next question to ask is: \emph{Where do I study Computer Science?}
\cite{oya2015university, cmp2018students, baharun2010institutional} have found that students provide a variety of reasons for their college choice, the most prominent reasons include:
\emph{prestige} or \emph{reputation} of the college, 
\emph{location} or \emph{practicality}, 
\emph{friends} attending the college,
\emph{departments} or \emph{academic staff},
\emph{family members} being college alumni,
and particular \emph{sports clubs} or \emph{societies}.
That being said, there seem to be cultural differences 
in priorities towards college choice.
For instance, in \cite{baharun2010institutional}, a study of Malaysian college students, the college's \emph{reputation} was the most important criteria
in their college choice justification , 
whereas in \cite{telli2006university}, a study of Turkish college students, \emph{departments} and \emph{academic staff} were the most important criteria
in their college course justification.
While we can clearly observe cultural differences in the most important criteria for college choice,
a similar point stands:
just because Malaysian students, for example, value college reputation as the most important criteria in college choice, 
does not imply Malaysian students do not value other aspects to the college when reasoning about their college decision, 
as can be found in \cite{baharun2010institutional}.\\
